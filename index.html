Why Do Some Colleges Graduate Far More Students Than Others?
College graduation rates vary dramatically across the United States. At some institutions, nearly every student finishes their degree, while at others, fewer than half graduate within six years.

What explains this gap?

In this interactive article, we explore several factors that may shape graduation outcomes — including institution type, tuition, faculty resources, and admissions selectivity — using data from more than 1,200 colleges across the United States.
A First Clue: Public vs. Private Institutions
A simple place to start is the type of institution. Do private colleges graduate more students than public universities?

Across our dataset, private institutions do appear to have higher average graduation rates. On average, about 60.8% of students graduate from private colleges within 150% of the expected time, compared with 49.7% at public institutions.

But this comparison raises an important question: is the difference really about institution type — or about other factors that tend to differ between public and private schools?
Looking Beyond Averages
Average values can hide a lot of variation.

When we look at individual colleges, the picture becomes more complicated. Each dot in the chart represents a single institution.

While private colleges tend to cluster at higher graduation rates overall, there is substantial overlap between the two groups. Many public universities graduate students at rates comparable to — or even higher than — some private institutions.

This suggests that institution type alone cannot fully explain graduation outcomes.
Does Spending More Lead to Higher Graduation Rates?
If institution type does not fully explain graduation outcomes, perhaps resources do.

One obvious difference between colleges is cost. Tuition and total attendance costs often reflect the level of resources available to support students — including facilities, academic services, and support programs.

When we compare average cost with graduation rate, a clear pattern begins to appear.
Interpreting the Pattern
Higher-cost schools tend to show higher graduation rates, and the relationship is moderately strong (r ≈ 0.66).

However, tuition alone is unlikely to fully explain the pattern. More expensive institutions often differ in many other ways — including student preparation, admissions selectivity, and institutional resources.

To better understand what might drive these patterns, we next look at a factor related to learning environments: faculty availability.
Do Smaller Classes Make a Difference?
Student–faculty ratio is often used as a proxy for class size and access to instructors. Schools with more faculty per student may provide more personalized academic support.

However, when we compare faculty ratio with graduation outcomes, the relationship appears relatively weak. Although schools with more full-time faculty sometimes show slightly higher completion rates, the variation across institutions remains large.

This suggests that instructional resources alone cannot fully explain why some colleges graduate more students than others. Another possible explanation is the preparation of incoming students.
Selectivity and Student Preparation
Admission rate provides a useful proxy for institutional selectivity. Schools that admit a smaller share of applicants are often enrolling students with stronger academic preparation.

When we examine admission rates alongside graduation outcomes, a clear pattern emerges: more selective institutions tend to show higher graduation rates.

This suggests that student preparation may play an important role in shaping graduation outcomes — alongside institutional resources.
How Do These Patterns Vary Across the Country?
Finally, we can look at how graduation outcomes vary geographically.

Average graduation rates differ noticeably across regions of the United States. Regions such as New England and the Mid-Atlantic show higher overall completion rates, while other regions fall lower on average.

These regional differences likely reflect a combination of factors — including the mix of public and private institutions, levels of institutional funding, and differences in admissions selectivity.
What Shapes College Graduation Outcomes?
No single factor fully explains why some colleges graduate far more students than others.

Several institutional characteristics show relationships with graduation outcomes, including institution type, tuition levels, faculty resources, admissions selectivity, and regional context.

Among these, admissions selectivity appears most strongly associated with graduation rates. Overall, student success likely reflects a combination of institutional resources, student preparation, and broader educational context.
